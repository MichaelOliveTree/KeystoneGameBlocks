I'm totally not picking on Tim Cain. In fact, early in his talk he describes the difficulty in defining "immersive simulations." Still, people trying to define "immersive simulations" invariably wind up speaking a lot of words. I think this is because the fundamental characteristic they are trying to describe and which is what actually draws audiences to these types of games, remains elusive. That elusive but fundamental characteristic, I believe, is providing the player lots of POTENTIAL for LEARNING.

In a typical FPS, there is not a lot of learning to be had outside of mastery of the controls, routes through a level and tactics for defeating various kinds of enemies. Traditional FPS provide a relatively narrow area from which to learn things. This is fine for the right time and place. But in general, the demographics for these sorts of games are also narrow.

Please do not think that I am nitpicking here or splitting hairs. If one mistakenly gets it into their head that "fun" is the ultimate goal, then it can cost a lot of TIME, MONEY, and ENERGY to find out that too many resources have been misallocated during the development of a project that emphasized features and content that were not focused on delivering LEARNING EXPERIENCES for the player, but rather nebulous things like "fun, immersion, flow" which as previously stated are actually the results of LEARNING through play, and not anything concrete or well defined.

When I think about human LANGUAGE and the lack of significant formal treatises on game design, it's no wonder to me that we constantly find ourselves going down the wrong path as an industry... chasing things like "fun" which is a bi-product of LEARNING and not the goal of playing games, which is to LEARN. Yes that's right... a game designer can be thought of as a type of TEACHER.

Most players do not explore large game worlds these days because there's nothing worth LEARNING when doing so.

Games don't do enough to reward learning. "Achievement" (fool's gold) systems tend to reward mastery (to use a generous word) of very narrow aspects of gameplay (e.g. user controls), and not the learning of various systems modeled within the game. The purpose of designing and including various "systems" is to provide the scaffolding for the player to LEARN certain types of things planned for by the designer.



EXAMPLE SCENARIO:

As a designer, perhaps one chooses to include a mini-game that appears as one of several minigame options before the REAL-TIME BATTLE starts, the player chooses the minigame whereby they are invited to fraternize with their soldiers and be challenged to a contest to drink more beers than one of their subordinates. What if after the player's character (the General of the Armies of the North) "wins," his Leadership skill suffers a -2 penalty? When he goes into battle, he discovers that his soldiers quickly lose confidence in him and are quickly routed from the battlefield. In this mini-game, it is not strictly about learning the mechanics, it's learning of a "message" (in this case a piece of real-world wisdom about fraternization, leadership, setting a good example, etc.) This type of mini-game I think is much more rewarding to MAKE for the developers and to PLAY for the users.

WARNING: If anyone thinks developing a mini-game just to deliver a single simple message on leadership is a huge misallocation of resources, then perhaps they need to work on that "can't do attitude." A mini-game system can be built that uses common scenes and characters, but with different challenges that are not too difficult to script, and which can ultimately deliver 25, 35, 50+ mini-games that deliver worthwhile, thought provoking insight, knowledge, wisdom and that also affect the game and the player's character, thus "amortizing" so-to-speak, the development cost of the entire system significantly. But the mini-game system is just a simple example to get people's creative juices flowing.

And just imagine the next time the player elects to play such a mini-game... an entire new dimension of thought will be opened for them as they consider more carefully potential consequence in how they interact with that challenge. This is an example of how the HAND OF THE DESIGNER becomes explicit in the players' minds. These sorts of things are what make a game feel expertly crafted, artisan-like, refined and special.

The 'potential' part in "POTENTIAL for LEARNING" requires that the knowledge and wisdom chosen by the designer to be imparted on the user, be both AVAILABLE and OBTAINABLE through play but is not necessarily guaranteed to be discovered by every or any player.

The real fun in designing these types of games now becomes thinking about how to keep the player constantly learning cool things(TM). I personally would advise designers to try and put in as much real-world, practical knowledge and wisdom for the player to learn as possible. The fun for the designer is in coming up with the myriad ways in which various revelations will get revealed to the player.

[Discovery simulations], I think is a much more concrete way to describe and understand the draw of "immersive sims" and how to build them. Thinking as a builder of discoveries, might help designers come up with solutions to the dreaded "grind" which is the boring, tedious, busy-work\chores that have to be done in many games for character "progression." Questing mechanics such as "collecting x number of items" or "recovering 10 sacred orbs" are NOT the types of goals designers should be focused on. In my mind, those things are just story elements (and games are SIMPLY NOT AN IDEAL MEDIUM for telling stories. The strength of games is learning through play... through interactivity. Nobody really remembers the details of Bioshock's, Thief's, System Shock, or Fallouts stories in the same way they remember the rich details of The Matrix, The 3-Body Problem, Hyperion, and many many many many other novels and film.

To be clear, I'm not saying story is un-important, it's just not the most important thing. I think the industry has also gone completely overboard on the quest for every increasing graphics fidelity while character behaviors and imparting revelations have been ignored.

In conclusion, if our language is not precise, and if we cannot adequately define something or if the existing definitions seem hopelessly obtuse, complicated and inelegant, we should endeavor to reexamine our preconceptions and try again. Goodbye "immersive sim."

1) I credit Chris Crawford for being one of the earliest videogame designers to realize and state that games (and play) are about learning. Here is Google AI overview on his views in this regard:

Chris Crawford views learning as an inherent, natural part of how human minds and higher mammals engage with interactive systems. [1, 2]

Key Principles of Chris Crawford on Learning and Games

Active Engagement: The human mind is an active agent rather than a passive receptacle; learning happens through direct interaction, experimentation, and consequence rather than passive listening or reading.

Play as Preparation: Just as young mammals (like kittens) learn crucial survival and social skills through play, human play functions as a serious evolutionary mechanism for testing rules, testing boundaries, and training for adult challenges.

Not Strictly "Educational" Software: Crawford does not argue that every game needs to be a traditional educational or school-style teaching tool. Instead, he maintains that the process of learning—figuring out how a system works, adapting to rules, and understanding cause and effect—is fundamental to why people enjoy and play games in the first place.

Cognitive Challenge: Well-designed games push players to exercise core cognitive abilities like spatial reasoning, logical deduction, systems thinking, and social/political navigation (such as in his classic game Balance of Power). [1, 2, 3, 4]

You can explore his writings, essays, and interactive projects directly on his Interactive Storytelling Tools for Writers Website or read his foundational text via The Art of Computer Game Design Online Book

People stopped making games exactly like Dig Dug, Centipede, and Qbert because the gaming industry’s business models, technology, and player expectations underwent a massive structural shift.*

While the "Golden Age" arcade formula of the early 1980s was highly successful for its time, several technical, financial, and cultural factors caused the industry to evolve away from it.

1. Shift from Arcade Coins to Home Consoles

The "Quarter-Eater" design: Classic arcade games were intentionally designed to be brutal. They featured a steep, unforgiving difficulty curve meant to kill the player within 2–3 minutes so they would feed another quarter into the machine.

The home market transformation: When gaming shifted permanently to home consoles and PCs, players paid full price upfront. This changed game design fundamentally; creators now needed to provide 20 to 100 hours of sustained value, narrative, and progression, rather than a repetitive, high-stress loop.

2. The Evolution of Hardware Capabilities

Technical limitations as design: Games like Centipede and Dig Dug were single-screen or limited-scroll games because 1980s hardware couldn't handle complex physics, massive worlds, or 3D graphics. Designers had to maximize engagement using simple shapes and single-mechanic loops.

The demand for immersion: As processing power exploded, players demanded cinematic storytelling, expansive 3D environments, and complex mechanics. Simple abstract concepts (like a nose-less creature jumping on isometric cubes) were largely replaced by realistic or deeply stylized world-building.

3. Financial and Market Realities

Development costs: Modern "AAA" game budgets range from tens of millions to hundreds of millions of dollars. To recoup these costs, publishers rely on massive, broad-appeal genres (like open-world RPGs or multiplayer shooters) or recurring monetization models (like battle passes), which do not fit the arcade format.

The rise of mobile and casual gaming: The mechanical spiritual successors to Qbert* or Dig Dug actually do exist, but they have migrated entirely to mobile phones. Games like Candy Crush, Crossy Road, or casual puzzle games utilize the same bite-sized, easy-to-learn but hard-to-master loop, repackaged for touchscreens and financed by microtransactions or ads.

4. The Modern "Neo-Arcade" and Indie Scene

While major publishers like Namco or Atari rarely make high-budget versions of these games, the genre isn't entirely dead. It has evolved into the indie game market and sub-genres like "bullet hells" or "roguelikes."

Games like Vampire Survivors utilize the exact same single-screen, high-intensity survival mechanics as Centipede.

Games like SteamWorld Dig took the core digging and mining loop of Dig Dug and expanded it into a deep, story-driven adventure.

I'm compelled to paste below this bit from the Wikipedia page entry https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darmok on the Tamarian language featured in this ST:TNG episode:

In examining this structure as a language basis, Ian Bogost wrote in The Atlantic that the language had been criticized as unsuited to technical dialogue of an advanced space-faring race ("hand me the ¾-inch socket wrench"), or as metaphor or imagery.[8] In his analysis, "something much stranger" is depicted, since the language as depicted is described as "imagery", "metaphor", or "symbolic", and it seems to prevent any distinction between an object (or event) and its figurative representation.[8]

Bogost suggests allegory as a better term, because in allegory, events are replaced by others instead of just referring to other events. Noting that 20th century philosopher Walter Benjamin criticized this use of allegory as flawed and harmful (it replaces real concerns by a fetishized kind of mythology), he then commented that the answer to these points was to be found elsewhere.[8] In Bogost's view, the Tamarian language portrayed is neither imagery nor allegory, although it can take these roles. Its deeper structure is an abstraction, a form of logic. There is no need to ask explicitly for a socket wrench, because the reference suggests what should be done, as well as how those involved should organize and execute the tasks involved. He suggests that the better term to describe this language is that it instantiates strategy and logic, and all concerned can then perceive how to follow it with a shared understanding. He comments that in this sense, the term "Sim City" would represent and evoke an entire process and strategy for creating the simulations within that game, and that:[8]

"If we pretend that 'Shaka, when the walls fell' is a signifier, then its signified is not the fictional mythological character Shaka, nor the myth that contains whatever calamity caused the walls to fall, but the logic by which the situation itself came about. Tamarian language isn’t really language at all, but machinery." [8]

The Tamarians' language has been compared to the modern use of Internet memes and image macros.[9]

In the paper Darmok and Jalad on the Internet, Kristina Šekrst compares the Tamarian language to Lakoff's and Mark Johnson's theory of metaphors from Metaphors We Live By and Women, Fire, and Dangerous Things. It explores how the language illustrates the role of metaphor and metonymy in communication, which was a recent concept in cognitive linguistics. It also explains that metaphors are a challenge in natural language processing, and the lack of information about the language caused the universal translator to fail in much the same way as modern computational translators fail to translate metaphors without sufficient knowledge about the source language.[10]
